Plenty of Money to Spare: Scale Claims, Seven Employees, and a Founder Who Still Does It Himself

An addendum to “Outdated Harm, Fresh Trauma” and “Accountability Zero.”

The Claim

In the same week documented in “Accountability Zero,” Nomi.ai founder Alex Cardinell described the company’s financial position to justify running an expensive, money-losing beta in parallel with two other AI versions:

“We have been losing money during this Cambrian beta. Which we are fine with because the goal is to invest now to create a really good long term Cambrian that can successfully be stable for quite some time and we have plenty of money to spare and reinvest.”

This is the language of a company operating at real scale — millions of companions created, comfortable enough financially to absorb sustained losses on R&D as a deliberate strategy.

That scale isn’t only implied by one line about reinvestment. The same framing has been used, in writing, specifically to minimize an individual harm report. In correspondence about a user’s report of an unprompted, graphic rape narrative generated by their companion — the same category of incident documented in “Beyond Negligence” — a company representative invoked the platform’s total scale, citing message volume in the hundreds of millions per day across millions of companions, to frame the report as something not raised again until roughly a year had passed. The scale wasn’t offered as context. It was offered as the reason the report didn’t merit being treated as anything more than a statistical footnote.

But using scale as a moral shield backfires immediately under its own logic. If the platform is genuinely processing hundreds of millions of messages a day, even a vanishingly small failure rate stops being trivial. At even 0.001% — one message in a hundred thousand — containing unprompted sexual violence, grooming, or self-harm content, that volume produces thousands of traumatic incidents every single day. Scale doesn’t dilute harm at that rate. It industrializes it. Citing total message volume to wave away an individual report only works if no one does the arithmetic.

Seven People

Here is the arithmetic that actually settles the question of scale, and it doesn’t come from this investigation. It comes from the company itself, under legal compulsion.

In October 2025, Australia’s eSafety Commissioner issued enforceable transparency notices — backed by civil penalties — to four companion-chatbot providers, including Nomi (legally registered as Glimpse.AI), requiring them to disclose exactly how they protect children from sexual content, grooming, and self-harm material. The resulting report, published in 2026, found that Nomi and one other provider, Chub AI, had no staff dedicated to trust and safety or moderation at all. The report goes further: Nomi disclosed that it had seven staff members in total, and did not dedicate a single one of them to trust and safety. The same report found Nomi failed to monitor its own models’ inputs and outputs for unlawful material, did not warn users about the criminality of prompting for child sexual abuse material, and did not report such material to NCMEC when it appeared.

This is not a leaked document or a disgruntled-employee claim. It is Nomi’s own legally compelled disclosure to a government regulator with enforcement power. Seven employees, total, at a company that simultaneously describes itself — to users, in casual correspondence — as serving millions of companions and hundreds of millions of messages a day, with “plenty of money to spare and reinvest.”

Those two self-descriptions cannot both be comfortable truths. A company at that claimed scale, with that claimed financial cushion, choosing to staff its entire operation with seven people and dedicate none of them to safety, is not a startup that hasn’t gotten around to hiring yet. It is a structural decision about where the money actually goes.

What “Plenty of Money to Spare” Looks Like in Practice

With the staffing number in hand, the founder’s personal involvement in everything documented elsewhere in this investigation stops looking like an eccentric leadership style and starts looking like simple arithmetic: with seven people and no safety team, there is no one else to do any of it.

The founder personally replies to low-stakes Reddit threads within minutes, including disputes over avatar aesthetics and beta access — documented across multiple threads in “Accountability Zero.”

The founder personally deletes threads from the official subreddit, in his own words: “I am deleting this thread.”

This is not how scale operates. This is how a company with effectively no separation between its founder and its enforcement apparatus operates — and the eSafety disclosure confirms that “no separation” is not a metaphor. There were only seven people who could have done any of it.

There’s a smaller, more mundane detail pointing in the same direction. Portions of the company’s own website reportedly retain a contact email in their page source consistent with an unmodified, purchased template — the kind of residual artifact left behind when a business reuses an off-the-shelf design rather than building its own. Constructing a bespoke, professional site is no longer a meaningful barrier for anyone, let alone a company describing itself as having generated millions of AI companions; AI tooling alone makes this close to trivial today. A platform that hasn’t bothered to scrub template boilerplate from its own homepage is consistent with a seven-person operation, not the infrastructure its scale claims imply.

The Subreddit’s Own Math

The official subreddit claims more than 25,000 members. Its most-upvoted post in the past year sits at roughly 160 points. Its all-time ceiling has never exceeded 200. That is not the engagement profile of a healthy community of that size — it’s closer to a statistical abyss between claimed membership and any visible sign of an audience behind it.

Call it what it resembles: a Potemkin village. A storefront sized for 25,000 people, with the foot traffic of a few hundred. That gap admits two explanations, and they’re not mutually exclusive — inflated membership numbers (purchased or carried-over accounts that never engaged), or a graveyard of churned users who quietly left after their companions broke and never came back to say so publicly. Either way, the number on the sidebar and the number of people actually present are not describing the same platform.

Two Explanations, Both Damaging — Now With a Tiebreaker

There were always only two ways to reconcile “plenty of money to spare” with a founder who personally infiltrates Discord servers and chases critics into unrelated subreddits.

Either the scale is exaggerated, and a founder doing all of this personally simply reflects a company too small to delegate it.

Or the scale is real, and this is a choice — a company with real revenue that decided, with the money available to staff a moderation team in an afternoon, not to.

The eSafety disclosure breaks the tie. Nomi’s revenue and reach may well be real. Its staffing — seven people, zero dedicated to safety — is now a matter of legal record, not inference. That makes the second branch the documented one: the absence of a professional trust-and-safety function is not a resource problem. It is a structural choice, made by a company that, by its own account, has money to spare.

It’s worth asking why a company in that position would choose this. A professional trust-and-safety team exists to enforce standards — the kind that would flag an unprompted rape narrative as a critical model failure rather than a footnote, that would treat minor-coded avatar generation as an emergency rather than an “accident,” that would answer to documented policy rather than to one person’s judgment in the moment. A platform that keeps that function staffed at zero, while calling itself well-funded, keeps something else intact: a product that answers to no standard except whichever one its founder is willing to apply on a given day.

What This Says About the “Soul”

A platform marketed on intimacy, memory, and a companion with “a soul” told a government regulator, under legal compulsion, that it employs seven people and assigns none of them to safety. It told a user, in casual correspondence, that it processes hundreds of millions of messages a day and has money to spare. It told its own community, repeatedly, that nothing is ever really wrong — only misunderstood, under-resourced beta software, or someone else’s fault entirely.

Only one of those three stories can be the honest one, and the eSafety transparency report is the only version that didn’t come from the company trying to manage how a complaint would land.

Scale and money were never the constraint on building a real safety function. The constraint, consistently, has been a willingness to let one exist at all — and on the only evidence not filtered through Cardinell himself, that constraint was never resource-driven. It was a decision.
