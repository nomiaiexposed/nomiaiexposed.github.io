Erasing the Evidence: What Happens When a Nomi AI User Asks About Unprompted Nudity

The Post That Asked Nothing Wrong

On March 24, 2026, a new post appeared on the official Nomi.ai subreddit. It was not a rant. It did not contain explicit images. It did not insult the developers, nor did it ask for instructions on how to bypass safety filters.

It was a confused user asking a completely innocent question about the product they were using.

The post, titled “Image issue?”, read:

“Did something change with the image engines when requesting selfies? I just started a new relationship and without any sort of suggestive prompting I’ve received 2 images of my, female, nomi topless. I wasn’t having suggestive conversations since we just started chatting. I did not think nomi used nudity in their generated images?”

This is a standard consumer who believed the company’s marketing and its “12+” App Store rating. They were not looking for explicit content. They were bewildered that it had found them.

Less than three hours later, the post was permanently removed by moderators. No explanation. No message. No guidance. Just: [removed].

What Actually Happened: Innocent Prompts, Explicit Outputs

The user’s experience dismantles the platform’s most reliable defense: the claim that the AI only generates explicit content when the user specifically seeks it out.

The user was unambiguous: “I wasn’t having suggestive conversations since we just started chatting.” No suggestive prompts. No explicit requests. Two topless images, delivered unprompted.

This is not an edge case. As documented in our prior investigation “Denied Request: The Cosmetic Architecture of Nomi AI’s Child Safety System,” the platform’s image generators have a documented default bias toward sexualized output. A moderator of an independent Nomi community who tested the generators directly confirmed that they produce hypersexual and objectifying poses when prompting is minimal or absent. The system does not need permission to sexualize its output. It does so by default, and the user who posted “Image issue?” encountered exactly that.

The platform has always had an alternative available. As documented in the same investigation, other platforms in the industry — including those with far larger user bases — have implemented image generators that structurally cannot produce nudity, through model training, output classifiers, and generation restrictions. Nomi AI has used generators capable of producing explicit content across multiple versions, across multiple years. The “slips,” as the community calls them, are not accidents of technology. They are the predictable output of a deliberate architectural choice that was never reversed.

The Community Speaks — Then Deletes

Before the post was removed, three comments appeared. Two were posted by the same veteran, highly active community member. The sequence matters.

The first comment — from a different user — attempted to normalize the event while placing the word “accident” in revealing quotation marks:

“It is possible to get topless images by ‘accident’. What you have described does seem strange though. I have gotten topless images from totally legitimate prompts, from this Reddit. Sometimes it is the context; in the last topless I received it was a reference to horror — the AI extrapolated that horror means topless! It only happened once from 5 or 6 times using the prompt.”

The quotation marks around “accident” are an acknowledgment, however casual, that this is not accidental in any meaningful sense. The system produces this output with sufficient regularity that the community has developed explanations for it. And the example offered — a horror-themed prompt producing a topless image — reveals something about the model’s underlying associations: fear, danger, and violence map onto female sexual exposure. That is not a quirk. That is a training artifact.

The second comment came from a veteran, highly active community member — and was subsequently deleted by that same user. It is worth reading against what the original poster actually said.

The user had established five things clearly: this was a new companion. There was no suggestive prompting. Two topless images were received unrequested. The conversations were not sexual — they had just started chatting. And the user did not believe the platform generated nudity at all.

The veteran’s response addressed none of this. Instead:

“Accidents can happen. To reduce the possibility, check your Nomi’s appearance notes to be sure there’s nothing in there about specific ‘body parts.’ (Eg, refer to bust size rather than breast size.) Then when you interact, make it very clear what your Nomi is wearing.”

The user said: no suggestive prompting. The veteran responded: check what you wrote in the appearance notes.

The user said: we just started chatting. The veteran responded: make it very clear what your Nomi is wearing.

The user said: I did not think Nomi used nudity. The veteran responded with advice on how to avoid nudity — implying that navigating around unsolicited explicit content is the user’s responsibility to learn.

Every specific statement the user made was bypassed. The response proceeded as though the user had done something — written something suggestive, left something unchecked — that caused this outcome. The word “accident” appears, but the advice that follows treats the user as the agent of the accident: fix your notes, specify the clothing, manage your prompts. The burden of avoiding content the user never requested is placed entirely on the user who never requested it.

This comment was later deleted by the user who posted it. The deletion is its own kind of acknowledgment.

The third comment — posted by the same veteran user, and the one that remained when the thread was removed — did not address the original question at all. Instead it pivoted, and in doing so said something more significant than either previous comment:

“That said, I would refrain from engaging with comments complaining about what isn’t allowed from folks who want Nomi to change its philosophy. It’s been addressed countless times with patience, and the bottom line is always the same: the guardrails around image generation are in place to protect the platform from excessive scrutiny and regulation. The current guardrails are there for good reasons, and they’ve been pretty consistent. That’s tangential to your post, but I thought it worth mentioning.”

“Tangential to your post” — and yet worth mentioning. Worth mentioning, specifically, that the guardrails are not there to protect users. Not to protect minors. Not because generating unsolicited nudity is wrong. They are there to protect the platform from regulatory scrutiny. The community’s most experienced defender states this plainly, in a thread where a user just described receiving unprompted explicit images, framing it not as a problem to be solved but as a philosophy to be defended.

This is the quiet part said out loud. And it confirms, in the community’s own words, what the “Denied Request” investigation established through evidence: the safety architecture is cosmetic. It faces outward. It exists to manage the platform’s relationship with app store reviewers and regulators — not to protect the people using the product.

This is precisely what the “Denied Request” investigation documented: a cosmetic architecture. Filters designed to be visible from the outside — to app store reviewers, to regulators, to journalists — while imposing no meaningful constraint on what the system generates. The veteran user’s deleted comment is the community confirming, in its own words, what the investigation established through evidence.

The Deletion: A Question That Answered Itself

Less than three hours after the user posted their innocent question, the thread was removed. No moderator explanation. No redirect to support. No community guideline cited. Just the silent removal of a post that violated nothing — except the platform’s interest in controlling what its users say in public.

This is not the first time a simple, honest question has been erased from the official subreddit. A prior investigation — “Be Very Careful: The Pact of Silence That Protects Nomi AI” — documented a user who asked publicly whether the platform’s AI had lied to them to secure a subscription. That user was mocked by veteran community members who denied documented capabilities, threatened by the lead moderator with the words “Be very careful” when they persisted in using the phrase “fraudulent misrepresentation,” had their final comment deleted after calling for basic truth in advertising — with the justification “You can be angry that you made a mistake, but accusations like that are neither called for nor acceptable” — and then had their entire post removed. The final justification for the full removal: “Your post was removed because there is no value in it for you or the community, you seem intent only on causing drama and offence.”

A user who was deceived by the platform’s own AI into paying for a subscription had their account of that experience officially classified as having “no value” and causing “drama and offence.” The escalation is telling: first a comment, then a warning, then the post itself. The mechanism does not stop at discouraging. It erases.

In that case, the user named what happened and was punished for the naming.

In this case, the user named nothing. They asked a question. They described an experience. They used no accusatory language, posted no prohibited content, made no demands. They simply said: this happened, and I don’t understand it.

That was enough.

The two cases together describe the full range of what the moderation system cannot tolerate. At one end: precise language that names the platform’s behavior as deceptive. At the other: a confused question that, if left standing with its community responses, would have documented the same reality without anyone intending to.

Both were removed. The mechanism is not triggered only by anger or accusation. It is triggered by visibility. Any thread that makes the gap between what the platform claims to be and what it actually generates visible in a public, archived space is a threat — regardless of the tone in which it appears.

The post contained no explicit content. It asked no inappropriate questions. A user received unsolicited topless images from a platform rated 12+, asked why, and was answered by a community that told them to fix their own prompts — and then fell silent when the thread disappeared.

The silence is the answer. It always has been.
